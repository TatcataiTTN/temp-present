Diffusion & Innovation Systems

From S-curve adoption to running an Innovation Management System — and the four assignments that go with it

1 · Innovation Diffusion

What is innovation diffusion?

Definition. Diffusion is the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels, over time, among members of a social system. It is distinct from invention (creating something new) and innovation (bringing it to practical use) — diffusion is what happens after that, as the market actually adopts it.

The S-Curve of Diffusion

Three phases, one curve

Worked example from the lecture

The S-curve in Vietnam: smartphone adoption

Phase 1: Emergence (2008–2010)

slow start, market penetration

First smartphones (original iPhone, early Android HTC) appear in Vietnam. Devices are very expensive, 3G is in its infancy, most people still use "brick phones."

Phase 2: Growth (2012–2018)

explosive, mass adoption

Affordable Xiaomi, Oppo, Samsung A-series phones flood the market. Nationwide 3G/4G plus Zalo, Facebook, Grab make a smartphone a necessity for nearly everyone.

Phase 3: Maturity (2020–present)

saturation and stability

Almost every adult owns a smartphone. Few first-time buyers remain; competition shifts to the high end, or to the next upgrade cycle.

Everett Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations

Five adopter categories

Tidd & Bessant

5 attributes that drive the rate of adoption

1. Relative Advantage

Is it significantly better than existing solutions — on price, speed, convenience?

2. Compatibility

Does it fit existing values, habits and technical infrastructure?

3. Complexity

Is it hard to understand or use? Higher complexity → slower adoption.

4. Trialability

Can users experiment with it on a limited or free basis first?

5. Observability

Are the results and benefits easily visible to others?

4 · Innovation Management in Enterprises

Why manage innovation at all?

The necessity

  • Align innovation with strategic direction, resources, metrics
  • Keep strategy flexible as opportunities change
  • Balance exploiting today’s process with exploring new ones
  • Remove barriers and mindsets that stifle initiative
  • Ground innovation in real market and customer needs

The roles it plays

  • Focuses the enterprise on its most critical innovation activities
  • Lets top management define a vision and allocate resources
  • Builds a shared, organization-wide awareness of innovation work
  • Surfaces bottlenecks through assessment
  • Integrates with the firm’s other management systems
Innovation Management Systems (IMS)

The fundamental elements, and 7 evaluation principles

4 fundamental elements

  • Context of the organization: internal/external issues relevant to objectives
  • Leadership: vision, strategy, policy, roles
  • Planning: activities that address opportunities and risks
  • Support: people, finance, tools, IP strategy

7 evaluation principles

  • Increasing value for the business
  • Challenging goals & strategies
  • Mobilizing business development
  • Focusing on the future
  • Relevance to context, best practice
  • Flexibility & comprehensiveness
  • Effectiveness & reliability

Exercise 1, deep case, continued

Grab: the three systems behind $22.1B in GMV

EXP: the Search→Capture loop, running monthly

  • Internal A/B-testing system: drivers/riders randomly split into groups, old vs. new matching or pricing logic compared on real outcomes
  • Hundreds of experiments run every month — fares and matching are continuously re-optimized on the same driver network, no new product launch needed
  • This is Capture Value treated as a repeatable monthly process, not a one-time launch event

The flywheel, proven in Grab’s own filed numbers

  • Incentive spend fell from 13.3% of GMV (2022) → 9.9% (2023) → ≈10% (2024), while monthly users grew 32.7M → 41.3M → 47.2M
  • FY2025 (filed Feb 2026): first-ever full-year net profit, $200M, on $3.37B revenue (+20%) and $22.1B GMV (+21%) — after losses of $485M (2023) and $158M (2024)
  • GrabMaps: built in 2022 purely to route Grab’s own drivers through motorbike alleys standard maps miss — then sold B2B once it worked. An innovation nobody set out to sell.

Exercise 1, deep case, the analysis

Why this is an IMS problem, not just PR

Sept 12–13, 2026: drivers in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City and Da Nang organized a two-day app log-off, coordinated through a 166,000-member driver community group. Multiple outlets (VnExpress, Bloomberg, AsiaNews) report commission deductions in the 30–50% range, above Grab’s stated 20–27%; exact per-ride figures vary by source and are disputed. Documented prior rounds: January 2018 (commission 20%→23.6%) and December 2020 — this is at least the third cycle of the same dispute.

The evaluation-principle reading

  • 2025 is the exact year the 7 evaluation principles’ first item — increasing value for the business — finally succeeded (slide 20)
  • The same filings show no comparable discipline applied to relevance to context: the course’s own principle meant to weigh local stakeholder impact

The transferable lesson

  • An IMS optimized on one evaluation principle while ignoring another doesn’t fail quietly — it produces exactly this kind of headline
  • “Role of innovation management” therefore includes knowing which principle a firm is currently skipping, not just which one it’s winning on

Exercise 2, step 2 — filter and justify

Why these 2 beat the other 8

Filter rule: attack the weakest attribute first. The scorecard below shows compatibility is EV’s only low score — so the winning ideas are the two that fix compatibility at the lowest cost per driver reached.

AttributeScore (today’s EV, e.g. VinFast)
Relative advantageMedium-high — lower running cost, but price and range still trail ICE

Pick 1: Battery subscription (not swap-at-gas-station)

Removes the single biggest compatibility cost — the battery itself — without needing a new physical network built from scratch. This is literally Selex/Xanh SM’s real strategy, not a hypothetical.

Pick 2: B2B fleets first, consumers second

One fleet contract reaches hundreds of daily riders as passengers, each one a free trial — fixing trialability and compatibility distrust at once, far cheaper than per-household marketing.

Exercise 3, part 1 — the researched reflection

Electric vs. gasoline, head to head: three moments

Around 1900, roughly 28–38% of American cars were electric — Detroit Electric and Baker Electric sold well, especially to urban and female drivers, because the electric car started instantly and had no gears to grind. By 1912, Ford’s Model T cost $650 against a $1,750 electric roadster. By the mid-1920s, nearly every electric-car maker had gone out of business. Not because the electric car got worse — because gasoline stopped being the harder car to use.

Exercise 3, part 2 — the human story

Bertha Benz, August 1888: advertising by doing

Karl Benz had invented the car two years earlier but struggled to sell it — the public simply didn’t believe it worked. His wife, Bertha Benz, took it the 100km to her mother’s town without telling him, buying fuel from a pharmacy because nowhere else sold it. It became the first real-world product demo in automotive history: proof before a single word of marketing.

Exercise 3, part 3 — the ~300-word write-up

Why early EVs failed to diffuse: the full reflection

Around 1900, the electric car was arguably the more advanced product: instant start, no hand-crank, no gears to grind, quiet and clean. Within two decades it had nearly vanished from the mass market, and the reason was not technology.

Compatibility was the deepest, most structural problem. Gasoline could be bought at a fast-growing number of stations along a road network the 1916 Federal Aid Road Act was actively expanding; home charging assumed a reach into American households that, outside cities, simply did not exist yet. Thomas Edison spent roughly a decade, 1901–1910, trying to solve exactly this with a better nickel-iron battery — even that could not close the range gap in time.

Meanwhile relative advantage flipped. Ford’s moving assembly line, a process innovation, cut the Model T’s price from $825 in 1908 to roughly $260 by the mid-1920s, far below any electric car; cheap oil made running cost irrelevant; and Kettering’s 1912 electric starter erased gasoline’s one real weakness, closing the complexity gap that had favored EVs. And Bertha Benz’s 1888 drive had already done, for free, what no gasoline-car ad could: proven in public, over real distance, that the thing worked.

The lesson generalizes beyond cars: a technologically advanced product fails in diffusion when an incumbent closes the gap on its one weak attribute (complexity, for gasoline) while the newcomer’s own weak attribute (compatibility, for electric) is structural and expensive to fix. Being better is not enough if the market cannot yet use what you offer — and cannot yet see, with their own eyes, that it works.

Exercise 4, part 1

"Electric Vehicles: The Second S-Curve" — Intro & Overview, with real figures

1. Introduction

Problem statement: Round one is documented history — EVs held roughly a quarter to a third of the 1900 US market, then nearly every electric-car maker had gone out of business by the mid-1920s, beaten on compatibility, not technology.

Why now: VinFast launched Vietnam’s first mass-market EV, the VF e34, in 2021; lithium-ion cell costs have fallen sharply over the past decade — the first time since 1912 the compatibility gap is closing instead of widening.

2. Overview

Type: Product innovation (battery + motor) + Business Model innovation (battery-as-a-service — Xanh SM/Selex, both Session 2 cases).

Level: Disruptive to a 110-year-old refueling habit; rollout itself is incremental, city by city.

Target users: fleets and urban commuters first — Xanh SM put VinFast EVs into taxi service directly, skipping the individual-buyer’s compatibility doubt entirely.

Exercise 4, part 2

Core Analysis, Impact & Challenges, Conclusion

3–4. Core analysis, impact & SWOT

Value proposition: round-two directly attacks round-one’s killer (compatibility) via swap/subscription, while keeping round-one’s real edge (simplicity, instant torque).

  • S: closes the exact historical gap that killed EV round one — not a new, unproven bet
  • W: heavy upfront CapEx for batteries/stations — Selex’s own case names this as its top risk
  • O: urban air-quality policy pressure + falling battery cell cost curve
  • T: incumbents could close the gap again — Kettering’s starter did it to EV once already in 1912

5. Conclusion

Summary: round two succeeds only to the extent it actually fixes compatibility — relative advantage alone already lost once, in 1912.

Key lesson, stated as a rule: when reviving a technology that failed before, diagnose which of the 5 attributes killed it the first time, and fix that one — do not assume the technology improving is sufficient by itself.

Wrap-up

Diffusion is not automatic — it has to be managed

Technological excellence alone does not guarantee success; commercial victory relies on market adoption. Managers must actively design products and strategies for each adopter segment, not assume the S-curve will climb itself.
